> **Why this page is structured this way:** The user-visible question "why was my order rejected" and the inspection question "show me your pre-trade controls" are the same question asked from two directions, and both are answered by one artefact: an ordered, sourced, complete list of checks. So this page is that list. It is grouped by the kind of question each check asks rather than by the system that performs it, because grouping by system hides the fact that a single logical control is sometimes split across the OMS and the RMS. Each check carries its source, because an inspector's first question about any control is whether it is mandated or chosen.

## TL;DR

- **Forty-two checks in eight groups**, every one of which must either pass or produce a specific, actionable rejection. A check that silently passes because its input was unavailable is the failure mode this page exists to prevent.
- **Source matters more than placement.** A regulatory check traces to a circular, an exchange check traces to exchange rules and must be refreshed, a discretionary check traces to an approved policy. Misclassifying a discretionary leverage policy as a regulatory requirement is the most common error, and it is one an inspector notices immediately.
- **Order is a design decision with reasons**: identity, then static facts, then client status, then instrument restrictions, then price, then quantity bounds, then the credit decision, then behaviour. Cheap before expensive, and deterministic before stateful.
- **Nine checks can never be waived** — they are regulatory or exchange-mandated, and a waiver mechanism that can reach them is itself a defect.
- **The commonly omitted checks are the interesting ones**: ban-period enforcement on *fresh* positions only, surveillance-measure application, self-trade prevention, employee and connected-person restrictions, unresolved screening alerts, and demat-active-before-delivery-trade.
- **Every rejection must name the check.** Internally by code, externally in language the client can act on. A generic "order rejected" destroys both the client experience and the audit trail.
- **A check whose input is stale must reject, not pass.** Fail-closed, scoped as narrowly as the missing fact allows.

## Conceptual overview

A pre-trade ladder is a sequence of questions about an order, arranged so that the cheapest and most certain are asked first. That ordering is not only a performance optimisation — it determines the quality of the answer the client receives. Telling someone their order has insufficient margin when it was actually for a contract that does not trade today is technically a rejection and practically a misdirection. Each check in the right position produces the right explanation, which is why the order of the ladder is a deliberate design artefact rather than an implementation accident.

The deeper purpose of the ladder is that it is the firm's demonstration that it exercises control. An inspection does not ask whether a broker is careful; it asks what the controls are, where they sit, what evidences their operation, and what happens when they fail. A documented ladder with a source against every rung answers all four. The absence of such a document means the answer has to be assembled from code, which is slow, incomplete and unconvincing. This is why the classification column is not bureaucratic decoration: it is the evidence trail.

The third idea concerns completeness. Most brokers implement most of these checks. The ones that get missed share a characteristic — they protect against a situation that has not yet happened to that firm. Ban-period enforcement matters only when a client tries to open a fresh position in a banned underlying. Self-trade prevention matters only when two of a client's orders meet. Employee-restriction checks matter only when an employee trades a restricted scrip. Each is rare, each is cheap, and each is an inspection finding and a potential enforcement matter when it is absent.

## 1. Sources

| Code | Meaning | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| **R** | Regulatory — mandated by regulation or SEBI circular | The instrument and paragraph |
| **E** | Exchange — mandated by exchange or clearing corporation rules | The circular, plus a named refresh job |
| **D** | Discretionary — the firm's own policy | A board or committee-approved policy and an approver |

<Aside type="caution" title="C9 and C10 are the checks most often missing">
An unresolved sanctions or politically exposed person alert that does not block trading means the firm has a screening process whose output nothing acts on. An employee trading a scrip on the firm's restricted list is a conduct matter that the firm's own system permitted. Both are cheap to implement — a flag on the client record consulted at order time — and both are serious when absent, because their absence means a control the firm claims to operate does not actually operate.
</Aside>

## 5. Group D — instrument restrictions

| # | Check | Source | Needs | Rejection message shape | Waivable |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | Underlying not in the ban period — **fresh positions only**; position reduction remains permitted | E | Ban-period list | "This underlying is in the ban period. Only position reduction is allowed." | **No** |
| D2 | Surveillance measure restrictions applied in their most restrictive known form | E | Measure lists | "This security is under a surveillance measure; [restriction] applies." | **No** |
| D3 | Broker block list | D | Configuration | "We are not accepting orders in this security at present." | Yes |
| D4 | Client-specific instrument restriction | D | Client configuration | Internal | Yes |
| D5 | Illiquid or periodic-call-auction treatment applied | E | Exchange lists | "This security trades in a periodic call auction." | No |
| D6 | Trade-for-trade or compulsory-delivery segment treatment | E | Master | "This security is in trade-for-trade; intraday is not available." | No |
| D7 | Product treatment legal for this instrument — for example no intraday in trade-for-trade | D and E | Master plus policy | As above | Partly |

The D1 distinction is the one that gets implemented wrongly: a ban period restricts the creation of *fresh* positions in derivatives on that underlying; it does not prevent a client from reducing an existing position. A blanket block is both over-restrictive and, for a client trying to exit, actively harmful.

## 8. Group G — the credit decision

| # | Check | Source | Needs | Rejection message shape | Waivable |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | Margin requirement computed on the post-trade position | R | [Margin engine](/broking-kyc/finix-broking-setup/rms/02-margin-engine/) | — computes | **No** |
| G2 | Recognised collateral valued at current haircuts | E | Collateral, haircut list | — computes | **No** |
| G3 | Clear cash balance, excluding unconfirmed receipts | R | Ledger | — computes | **No** |
| G4 | Available = collateral plus clear cash, less utilised, less blocked, times the applicable multiplier | D for the multiplier; R for the structure | All of the above | — computes | Multiplier only |
| G5 | **Requirement ≤ available** | R | G1 and G4 | "This trade requires ₹X; you have ₹Y available." | **No** |
| G6 | Cash-equivalent composition respected where it binds | E | Collateral composition | Internal | **No** |
| G7 | Unpaid-securities exposure not used to support new trading | R | CUSPA state | Internal | **No** |
| G8 | Margin-funded position limits where applicable | R | Funding framework | Per framework | No |

<Aside type="note" title="G5 is where the message quality is won or lost">
"Insufficient margin" is the least useful rejection a broker produces. The same rejection expressed as "this trade requires ₹X and you have ₹Y available" tells the client the size of the gap and lets them act — add funds, reduce quantity, or choose a different instrument. The information is already in the decision record; surfacing it costs nothing and removes a large share of support contacts. Splitting the computation ([G1](#8-group-g--the-credit-decision)) from the comparison (G5) in the ladder is what makes it available.
</Aside>

## 10. Checks that are not in the ladder

Each of these belongs somewhere, and putting it in the pre-trade path is a design error.

| Check | Where it belongs | Why not pre-trade |
|---|---|---|
| Know-your-customer completeness | Onboarding, before activation | An activated client is a client whose KYC is complete |
| Periodic re-KYC due | Account lifecycle, with a status consequence | It changes client status; the ladder reads the status |
| Contract note and statement delivery | Back office | Post-trade |
| Settlement obligation computation | Back office | Post-trade |
| Suspicious transaction evaluation | Transaction monitoring, near real time | Pattern-based, not order-based; blocking an order is rarely the right response |
| Best execution demonstration | Smart order routing, where enabled | Routing, not acceptance |
| Client suitability profiling | Onboarding and periodic review | Sets client status |
| Upstreaming computation | End of day | Not an order-time concern |

## 11. Waiver and override

| Principle | Detail |
|---|---|
| Nine checks are never waivable | A2, A3, A4, A6, A7, B9, B10, C2, C4, C9, C10, D1, D2, E1, E2, G1, G2, G3, G5, G6, G7, H1, H2, H5, H10, H12 — the regulatory and exchange-mandated set. A waiver mechanism that can reach any of them is itself a defect |
| Waivable checks need authority | Per-order, time-bound, with a role distinct from the order placer |
| Every override is logged | Who, when, which check, which order, what reason, from a closed list |
| Overrides are reviewed | Weekly by risk, monthly by compliance; a rising count is a limit-setting problem, not an approval problem |
| Standing overrides do not exist | A check waived permanently is a check removed; remove it deliberately, with approval, rather than by habit |

## 12. Evaluation cost and ordering

| Group | Relative cost | Why it sits where it does |
|---|---|---|
| A identity | Negligible | Nothing can precede it |
| B static | Negligible | Catches the largest share of malformed orders |
| C client status | Low | Cached current-day state |
| D instrument restrictions | Low | List membership |
| E price | Low | One lookup, plus comparisons |
| F quantity bounds | Low | Bounded arithmetic, before any computation |
| **G credit** | **High** | Margin computation and limit resolution |
| H position and behaviour | Medium | Needs the post-trade position, so it follows G |

Any proposal to add a check must name the group it joins and the latency it costs. A check added without a budget allocation is a check that silently consumes someone else's.

## 13. Exception paths

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| A check's input is unavailable | **Reject**, scoped to what the missing input affects; never pass by default |
| A check is disabled in configuration | Treat as a change requiring approval; a disabled regulatory check is a reportable control failure |
| Rejection rate spikes on one check | Investigate as a configuration or data incident before assuming client behaviour |
| An exchange rejects an order the ladder should have caught | Defect report; the ladder has drifted from exchange rules |
| An override is requested repeatedly for the same client | The limit is wrong; fix the limit rather than approving overrides |
| A new regulatory control is announced | Map it to a group, assign a number, implement, and update this list; the list is the artefact that must stay current |
| A check fails open due to a code path | Severity one; quantify the orders released during the window |

## Practical notes

- **Keep this list as a living document with a version.** It is the single most useful artefact in an inspection of pre-trade controls, and a stale version is worse than none.
- **Name the check in every rejection, internally.** The code is how you find the drift; the client message is how they fix the order.
- **Classify honestly.** Calling a discretionary leverage policy regulatory is the error inspectors find fastest.
- **Implement D1 as fresh-positions-only.** A blanket ban-period block prevents clients from exiting, which is the opposite of the intent.
- **Build C9 and C10 even though they rarely fire.** Their absence is the finding, not their firing rate.
- **Review overrides weekly.** A rising override count is a signal about limits, not about approvals.
- **Add the check number to the decision record.** It makes "why was this rejected" a lookup rather than an investigation.
