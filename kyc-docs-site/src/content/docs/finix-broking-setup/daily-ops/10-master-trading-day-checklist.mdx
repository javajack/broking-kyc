> **Why this page is structured this way:** The rest of this plane splits the day into runbooks because each phase has its own logic and its own reader. But nobody actually operates a broking business one runbook at a time: the operations lead needs one surface that says what must happen today, who owns it, whether it has happened, and what to do when it has not. That surface is this page. It is deliberately a superset rather than a summary — every item from every runbook appears here, plus the items that fall between runbooks and are therefore owned by nobody, which is where real operations fail. The ordering is chronological because that is how the day is lived, and the day-type overlays are separate because the commonest operational failure in an Indian broking operation is running an ordinary-day checklist on an extraordinary day.

## TL;DR

- **This is the clockwork.** Roughly two hundred and sixty discrete activities on an ordinary trading day, before any day-type overlay. An operation that cannot enumerate them is relying on individual memory, which fails on leave days and during incidents.
- **Six gates stop the day.** Instrument master, risk parameters, ban list, surveillance lists, collateral and ledger load, and exchange sessions. Red on the master or the collateral load means do not open; red on the others means open with a stated scope of refusal.
- **The default on amber is no-go.** A delayed open costs reputation; a day run on wrong parameters costs money, penalties and an inspection finding. The asymmetry should be built into the decision, not re-argued each morning.
- **Every item has a named owner and leaves evidence.** "Operations" is not an owner. The gate status board, retained, is the record that the day opened properly, and it is the first artefact an inspector asks for after an incident.
- **The items between phases are the ones that fail.** Nothing in the begin-of-day runbook owns the overnight batch result; nothing in the end-of-day runbook owns tomorrow's expectation. Those seams are explicit here.
- **Day type is determined before the day starts, not discovered during it.** Settlement, expiry, tender, physical-settlement, post-holiday, month-end and daylight-saving overlays each add mandatory items, and several can coincide.

## Conceptual overview

A trading day is not one process but five concurrent ones with different clocks, and the checklist's job is to keep them visible simultaneously. There is the *market* process, which runs from session establishment to close and is bounded by exchange timings. There is the *money* process, which runs on banking hours and settlement cycles and whose deadlines mostly fall before the market's. There is the *record* process, which starts when the market closes and runs into the night. There is the *obligation* process, which operates on a settlement cycle and therefore concerns trades from previous days while today's are still being made. And there is the *compliance* process, whose deadlines are measured in days and weeks but whose inputs are produced today. Any checklist organised purely by clock time will interleave these confusingly; this one marks each item with its process so that an operator can read a single thread when they need to.

The second idea is that a checklist item is only useful if it states an *assertion* rather than an action. "Load the instrument master" can be marked complete by someone who ran a job that failed. "The order management system reports today's business date and a record count within range" cannot. Every item below therefore has a testable assertion, and where the assertion is about another system's state it is phrased as a query to that system rather than a report from the job that fed it. This distinction is what separates a checklist that catches problems from one that merely documents intentions, and it is the single most valuable design decision on this page.

The third idea concerns abort behaviour. Each item's failure has a defined consequence, and those consequences are of four kinds: *stop the day*, *reduce the scope of the day*, *continue degraded with a stated compensating control*, or *continue and raise an exception for later*. The point of writing these down in advance is that the decision at eight in the morning becomes a lookup instead of a debate, and the person on shift does not need the authority to invent a policy. Where the correct response genuinely requires judgement, the item names the role whose judgement it is, and that role is reachable by phone — a checklist whose escalation path is an unanswered email is a checklist with a gap at its most important point.

<Aside type="danger" title="C-0.12 is the item most often skipped and most expensive to skip">
Exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories publish circulars continuously, and a meaningful fraction take effect the next trading day — a changed margin rate, a new file format, a security moving into a surveillance category, a revised cut-off, a lot size change. There is no mechanism that pushes these into an operation's systems; somebody has to read them and decide what changes. When this item is skipped, the firm discovers the change through a rejection, a penalty or a reconciliation break, typically several days later, by which time the cause is hard to find. The control is mundane: a named person, every morning, with a written record of what was reviewed and what was actioned, and a monthly check that the review happened on every trading day. This is also the mechanism that catches an unannounced file format change before it arrives rather than after.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="C-3.27 is the hardest deadline in the day and the least visible">
Everything about the market's rhythm points at the exchange's pay-in time, but the firm's actual deadline is the bank's instruction cut-off for the relevant rail, which is earlier and differs by account and by rail. An operation that works to the exchange time will eventually discover that the bank stopped accepting instructions twenty minutes ago. The fix is to publish, internally, a single internal funding deadline derived from the earliest applicable bank cut-off minus a buffer, and to have risk, dealing and operations all working to that number rather than to the exchange's. The corresponding treasury decision point is C-3.26, which must therefore sit comfortably before it.
</Aside>

## 10. The go or no-go decision

| Element | Position |
|---|---|
| Owner | A named person on the rota with the authority to stop |
| Input | The gate status board: G1 to G6, each green, amber or red |
| **Default on amber** | **No-go**, or open in a reduced scope with the exception explicitly recorded |
| Red on **G1** instrument master or **G5** collateral and ledger load | **Do not open** |
| Red on **G2** parameters, **G3** ban list, **G4** surveillance or **G6** sessions | Open with the stated scope of refusal applied |
| Recording | Decision, time, owner, gate status, accepted exceptions, scope communicated |
| Communication | Clients and the exchange informed promptly where the open is delayed or scoped |
| Escalation | Head of operations and head of risk; both reachable by phone |
| Review | Every no-go and every scoped open reviewed within the week for root cause |

## 11. Day-type overlays

Each overlay adds mandatory items to the base checklist. Two or more frequently coincide.

| Overlay | Adds |
|---|---|
| **Settlement day** | Obligation files for the settling cycle; funds and securities pay-in before the cut-off; pay-out processing; short-delivery identification; auction participation where applicable; close-out computation |
| **Pre-holiday** | Funding across the holiday gap; extended settlement implications; client communication on altered cut-offs; larger treasury buffer |
| **Post-holiday** | Multiple days of accumulated parameter and reference files applied in the correct order; higher transaction volumes; a longer begin-of-day window; explicit supersede-or-accumulate decisions per file |
| **Equity derivatives expiry** | Settlement price capture; exercise and assignment processing; expiry margin treatment; position squaring reminders to clients in advance; the evening expiry-processing sequence |
| **Stock derivatives physical settlement** | Delivery obligation computation from derivatives; the escalating delivery margin through expiry week; advance client warning and forced square-off for clients unable to deliver or take delivery; netting against the cash segment |
| **Commodity tender or delivery day** | Delivery intention marking before the deadline; allocation processing; warehouse and receipt handling; delivery-period margin |
| **No-delivery period** | Altered settlement handling for the affected security; client communication |
| **Corporate action record date** | Entitlement computation; contract adjustment; collateral re-eligibility check on the resulting security |
| **Month end** | Periodic client reporting; charge and interest cycles; statement runs; running-account settlement where it falls due |
| **Quarter or year end** | Periodic regulatory reporting; net worth computation; statements; tax artefacts; audit support |
| **Daylight-saving changeover** | Commodity evening session close-time change applied and verified |
| **Mock trading or drill day** | A parallel non-production manifest; no production impact; results recorded as evidence |

<Aside type="caution" title="Two overlays that interact badly">
A physically settled stock derivatives expiry that falls in a month-end week during a no-delivery period on one of the underlyings is not a hypothetical; it happens. The failure mode is that each overlay's items are individually remembered and their interaction is not — the delivery obligation is computed, the month-end statement run is scheduled, and nobody notices that the no-delivery period changes the settlement treatment of one of the deliveries. The practical defence is that the day-type determination at C-0.01 produces a single combined checklist rather than several lists, and that an operations lead reviews the combined list for interactions before the day starts rather than during it.
</Aside>

## Practical notes

- **Generate the day's list; do not print this page.** The value is in the day-type overlay and the named-owner resolution, both of which are computed.
- **Review completion daily, not weekly.** An item that was not done is a decision someone made implicitly; the review makes it explicit and gives it an owner.
- **The seams are C-0.08, C-5.18 and C-5.31.** Each hands something from one day to the next, each is owned by the day that produces it rather than the day that needs it, and each is therefore the kind of item that gets dropped. Watch them.
- **Drill the gates.** For each blocking gate, rehearse the absence of its input in the mock environment at least twice a year, and keep the fallback decision current.
- **Resist shortening the list.** The pressure to trim comes from operators who know the routine, and the list exists for the days when the person who knows the routine is not there.
- **Count scoped opens and no-gos.** A rising count is a file-pipeline or vendor problem expressing itself as operational drag, not an operations problem.
