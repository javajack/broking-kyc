> **Why this page is structured this way:** There are two different questions hiding inside "who does what in a broking firm", and conflating them produces an org chart that satisfies nobody. The first is *who is personally accountable to a regulator* — a short list of named appointments with statutory consequences, intimation requirements and qualifications. The second is *who does the work* — a set of desks whose names are a matter of the firm's choice but whose functions are substantially determined by the rulebook. So this page separates them: sections 2 and 3 cover the appointments and their certification, section 5 covers the desks and anchors each one's job description in the obligations that create it. Section 7 states plainly where the regulation mandates a function and leaves the structure open, because inventing a mandate is as unhelpful as missing one.

## TL;DR

- **Four appointments carry personal regulatory accountability**: the compliance officer, the principal officer and designated director under the money-laundering rules, and the designated director under the 2026 broker regulations. Each has an intimation requirement and none of them is a title you can leave vacant.
- **Three more appointments apply only to designated large brokers**: a chief compliance officer, a chief risk officer and a chief information security officer, each at chief-officer level.
- **Every broker needs a cyber "designated officer" and a technology committee**, regardless of size. These are widely missed because they are not styled as chief-officer roles.
- **Certification is per function, not per title, and expires.** Every certificate runs three years and is revalidated either by re-examination before expiry or by a one-day continuing-education programme in the twelve months before it lapses. The firm, not the individual, carries the duty to ensure that happens.
- **There is no mandated headcount or shift pattern.** The regulation requires adequacy and the clocks do the rest; this page states the clocks and declines to invent the numbers.
- **Two separations everyone assumes are mandated are not** — dealing from risk, and the payout maker from the payout approver. They are required in substance through the internal-control and fraud-prevention obligations, and should be presented that way rather than as named rules.

## Conceptual overview

The regulatory architecture for people in a broking firm works by attaching obligations to functions and then requiring that somebody be identifiably responsible for each. It rarely specifies structure. This is why two firms of similar size can have very different organisation charts and both be compliant, and also why a firm can have an impressive chart and still fail an inspection — the question asked is never "do you have a head of operations" but "who approved this, on what authority, and can they show the certificate that entitled them to". The register below is therefore organised around accountability and evidence rather than reporting lines.

The second observation is that the accountability is personal in a way that is easy to under-appreciate. The compliance officer is expected to report non-compliance independently to the exchange, which means the role is structurally in tension with the management it sits inside, and the regulation protects that tension deliberately. The principal officer under the money-laundering rules must have access to senior management at the next reporting level or to the board, for the same reason. The designated director is the regulator's resident accountable principal and signs things that bind the firm. Designing these roles as ordinary managerial positions, reporting in the ordinary way, with ordinary performance incentives, defeats the purpose that the appointment exists to serve.

The third point is about certification as an operational constraint rather than a human-resources formality. A dealer whose certificate has lapsed is not a slightly non-compliant dealer; they are a person who may not place orders, and the identifier they use should be disabled. That makes the certification register a live operational dependency of the dealing function, which is why it appears as a begin-of-day gate in [the master trading-day checklist](/broking-kyc/finix-broking-setup/daily-ops/10-master-trading-day-checklist/) rather than as a quarterly compliance review. The commonest version of this failure is not a forgotten certificate but a forgotten *revalidation*, because the original examination felt like the event and the three-year clock did not.

## 2. The statutory and regulatory appointments

| Role | Accountable for | Appointment and intimation | Qualification | Expected reporting line |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Compliance officer** | Monitoring compliance with the securities law and the exchange rules; investor grievance redressal; approving market-related news before it is disseminated | A change is a material change, intimable to the regulator **through the exchange**; the name and contact must appear on the firm's website; a depository-side change is updated through the depository's own channel | Securities intermediaries compliance certification, the non-fund variant | Reports non-compliance **immediately and independently to the exchange** |
| **Principal officer** under the money-laundering rules | Identifying and assessing potentially suspicious transactions; onward reporting to the financial intelligence unit; the central point of contact for anti-money-laundering matters | Name, designation and contact details intimated to the unit's director, and any change; registration on the reporting platform | Must be **an officer at management level** | Must have access to, and be able to report to, senior management at the next reporting level **or the board** |
| **Designated director** under the money-laundering rules | Overall compliance with the money-laundering obligations | Intimated to the unit's director; registered on the reporting platform | The managing director, or a duly authorised whole-time director, managing partner, proprietor, managing trustee or controller | Board-level accountability |
| **Designated director** under the 2026 broker regulations | Being the regulator's resident accountable principal; signing the cyber categorisation approval and margin-penalty undertakings; attending exchange default-escalation meetings | A registration condition. **At least one designated director resident in India for at least one hundred and eighty-two days in the financial year**; existing brokers were given a window into **July 2026**. A change is a material change intimable through the exchange | The managing director or authorised whole-time director for a company, managing partner for a firm, designated partner for a limited liability partnership, the proprietor for a proprietorship | Direct to the regulator through the exchange |
| **Cyber designated officer** — **every broker** | Assessing, identifying and reducing cyber risk; incident response; setting standards and controls under the cyber security policy | A senior official or management person designated by the broker | Not prescribed | Receives unusual-activity reports; reviews attack instances jointly with the technology committee |
| **Technology committee** — **every broker** | Half-yearly review of cyber policy implementation; approving the vulnerability-assessment report before submission; approving the system audit report and the action-taken report | Constituted by the board, partners or proprietor | "Comprising experts"; no named certification | Review placed before the board, partners or proprietor for action |
| **Investor grievance redressal function** | An exclusive complaints address; the grievance and dispute-resolution platforms; the investor charter; monthly complaint disclosure | An exclusive electronic mail address, designated and publicly displayed | Not separately prescribed; the operations and risk certification covers grievance work as an activity | The compliance officer, or a dedicated grievance division |
| **Authorised person oversight function** | Running the firm's own authorised-person and branch inspection programme, enhanced due diligence on appointment, and rapid escalation of assured-return or unauthorised-scheme incidents | Authorised-person details kept current in exchange records; quarterly inspection reporting through the member interface, **including nil declarations** | Not prescribed | Reports to the exchange quarterly; disciplinary action on the authorised person is the member's own duty |
| **Internal auditor** (an external appointee) | Half-yearly audit of internal controls, regulatory compliance, data security and grievance effectiveness; monitoring corrective steps on regulator and exchange inspection findings | A declaration of no interest and full particulars shared with the exchange; exchange empanelment required | A practising chartered accountant, company secretary or cost accountant; empanelment adds a compliance or financial-markets certification | Reports to the firm, placed before the board, then to the exchange with paragraph-wise comments — and **may report directly to the exchange if the firm does not cooperate** |

### Appointments that apply only to designated large brokers

| Role | Accountable for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Chief compliance officer** | All regulatory compliance activity of the firm | Designated at chief-officer level |
| **Chief risk officer** | Overall risk management across client-identity, operational, technology and general risk | Chief-officer level; the risk management policy is reviewed half-yearly and the risk committee reports to the exchange |
| **Chief information security officer** | All cyber security activity, incident response and control ownership | A board-level cyber committee oversees; the half-yearly empanelled system audit report goes to the exchange **with the cyber committee's comments** |

<Aside type="caution" title="Two role questions where the honest answer is that no mandate was found">
**There is no located regulatory mandate for a chief technology officer** in a broking firm. The obligations that a reader might expect to attach to such a role attach instead to the **technology committee** and the **cyber designated officer**. A firm should of course have senior technology leadership; it should not record it in the compliance register as a statutory appointment.

Similarly, **no "annual compliance certificate" obligation for brokers** was established. Several real periodic artefacts exist — the half-yearly internal audit report, the net worth certificate, the system and cyber audit reports, the quarterly authorised-person reporting — and documentation that invents a single annual compliance certificate creates a deliverable nobody owes and distracts from the ones they do. The genuine calendar is in [periodic submissions](/broking-kyc/finix-broking-setup/monitoring/03-periodic-submissions/).
</Aside>

## 3. Certification mapped to function

Certification obligations attach to **functions performed**, not to job titles, which is why one person may need two certificates and another in a similar-sounding role may need none.

| Certification | Who must hold it |
|---|---|
| **Securities intermediaries compliance (non-fund)** | Compliance officers and others engaged in the compliance function |
| **Depository operations** | Persons engaged by a registered depository participant in dealing or interacting with clients, dealing with clients' securities, handling grievance redressal, internal control or risk management, activities bearing on operational risk, or maintaining the related books and records. **Also required of anyone conducting the internal or concurrent audit of depository operations** |
| **Securities operations and risk management** | Persons of a broker, trading member or clearing member **involved in or dealing with assets or funds of investors, redressal of investor grievances, internal control or risk management, and activities bearing on operational risk** |
| **Equity derivatives** | Approved users and sales personnel in the equity derivatives segment |
| **Currency derivatives** | Approved users and sales personnel in the currency derivatives segment |
| **Interest rate derivatives** | Approved users and sales personnel trading interest rate derivatives |
| **Common derivatives** | A consolidated alternative that serves **in place of** the equity, currency and interest-rate derivative certifications. **It does not subsume commodity derivatives** |
| **Commodity derivatives** | Approved users and sales personnel in the commodity derivatives segment |
| **Research analyst**, and the associated-persons variant | Research analysts and persons engaged in research |
| **Investment adviser levels**, and the associated-persons variant | Investment advisers and persons associated with investment advice. From 2026, persons performing **only sales and other non-core services** need the newer associated-persons certification instead of both adviser levels; existing holders need it only before their current certificates expire |

### Validity and revalidation — the operational part

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Validity** | **Three years** from the date of grant or revalidation |
| Revalidation route one | Passing the relevant examination again **before** expiry |
| Revalidation route two | Completing a relevant **one-day continuing-education programme**, in person or online, **during the twelve months preceding expiry** |
| New validity | Three years from the expiry of the existing certificate; where a certificate is issued off a continuing-education programme, three years from the programme date |
| **The firm's duty** | The intermediary **must ensure** its associated persons participate in an approved programme within the validity period. This is the firm's obligation, not merely the individual's |
| Timing of first certification | Typically within a defined period of employment for new hires, and a longer transition for persons already employed when a category was first notified |

<Aside type="danger" title="A certification caution, and one apparent discontinuation">
Two things make the certification register go wrong. First, **the revalidation window is a window, not a deadline** — the continuing-education route must be used in the twelve months *before* expiry, so a certificate allowed to lapse cannot be revalidated that way and the person must sit the examination again. A register that tracks only the expiry date, and not the opening of the revalidation window, will produce avoidable lapses. Second, the certification historically accepted for the **signatory of a broker's internal audit report** appears **no longer to be offered for fresh certification**, while remaining valid for existing holders until their certificates expire, and remaining listed in older exchange circulars as a qualifying credential. Any page or policy that lists internal-audit qualifications should say this explicitly rather than directing a new appointee toward a certification they may not be able to obtain. Verify the current position with the certifying institute before appointing.
</Aside>

### Certification of audit professionals

A separate regime from associated-person certification.

| Audit | Required credential |
|---|---|
| **Internal audit** (half-yearly) | An independent qualified practising chartered accountant, company secretary or cost accountant, with no conflict of interest. Exchange empanelment adds: an independent practising accountant within the firm; a minimum firm audit experience, preferably in securities markets; **at least two partners at all times**; not debarred by any professional body or regulator; and a partner or employee holding the compliance certification or an equivalent financial-markets certification. For a **designated large broker**, the firm must have **at least five partners at all times, of which at least two are full-time** |
| **System audit** | A minimum experience in information-technology audit of securities-market participants covering the major scope areas; recommended professional credentials in information-systems audit, security management or security professional certification; no conflict of interest; directors or partners not related to any broker, its directors or promoters. **A maximum of three successive audits of the same broker** |
| **Algorithmic-trading system audit** | A system auditor holding one of the recognised information-systems audit or security credentials |
| **Cyber audit and vulnerability assessment** | An auditing organisation **empanelled with the national computer emergency response team**; preferably several years of financial-services information-technology audit experience; recognised audit or security credentials; **licensed tools**; a signed non-disclosure agreement, with **the audit data and report not leaving the jurisdiction**; a maximum of three consecutive years then a **two-year cooling-off**; and no consulting engagement with the firm in the preceding two years |
| **Depository-participant system audit** | An empanelled auditor, or an independent auditor holding a recognised information-systems audit credential |
| **Internal or concurrent audit of depository operations** | A practising chartered accountant, company secretary or cost accountant with no conflict of interest, **and** the depository operations certification or its continuing-education programme |

## 4. Auditor rotation

Rotation is prescribed and is one of the more mechanical things to get wrong.

| Auditor | Rotation rule |
|---|---|
| **Internal auditor** | An individual: a maximum of one term of five consecutive years. A firm: a maximum of two terms of five consecutive years. Then a **five-year cooling-off**. **No firm with a partner in common with an outgoing firm may be appointed for five years.** The five-year blocks run from a defined base financial year |
| **Statutory auditor** | The same architecture: one five-year term for an individual, two for a firm, five-year cooling-off, common-partner bar |
| **System auditor** | A maximum of three successive audits |
| **Cyber auditor** | A maximum of three consecutive years, then a two-year cooling-off |

## 5. The desks, and what the rulebook makes each one own

Desk names are the firm's choice. The *functions* below are substantially determined by specific obligations, which is why each row names the anchor rather than describing a generic job.

| Desk | What it owns | Why — the obligation that creates it |
|---|---|---|
| **Dealing** | Order entry, modification and cancellation for clients from approved terminals; capturing the client code on every order; voice-recorded order taking; strict separation of proprietary activity | The client code must be entered when placing orders; terminals only at specified places; terminals operated by qualified persons who are employees or remisiers; separate regulation of the proprietary terminal; voice recording of order placement |
| **Risk management** | Pre-trade controls; margin adequacy before release; intraday monitoring; risk-reduction-mode handling; the square-off policy; upfront and peak margin collection; collateral allocation | Collection and reporting of margins; verification of upfront collection; pledge and re-pledge; **daily disaggregated collateral reporting to client level**; monitoring of risk reduction mode |
| **Back office and settlement** | Trade file ingestion; obligation download; contract notes **within twenty-four hours**; daily margin statements; running-account settlement; pay-in and pay-out; direct pay-out of securities to the client's demat account | The electronic contract note and its twenty-four-hour clock; settlement of running account; direct pay-out of securities; the annual signed statement of funds and securities to all clients |
| **Depository operations** | Account opening; instruction-slip issuance and execution; electronic instructions; authority and demat-debit arrangements; pledge and margin pledge; freezes; closure; transmission; client data modification | The concurrent-audit scope list for depository operations is effectively this desk's job description |
| **Client onboarding** | Identity documents and their verification; in-person verification, electronic signature and identity-wallet routes; identifier verification; tax self-certification; upload to the registration agency **within three working days**; central registry upload; periodic re-verification; modifications | The identity-verification framework; centralisation of tax self-certification at the registration agencies; the registration-agency to central-registry upload; the account-opening simplification and its document index |
| **Funds and treasury** | Client-bank against own-bank discipline; correct account nomenclature; **upstreaming of client funds**; bank guarantees; current accounts across banks; refusal of third-party payments; no cash | Current accounts in multiple banks; guarantees created out of client funds; upstreaming; the mode of payment and delivery; account naming and tagging; reporting all existing and new bank and demat accounts to the exchanges |
| **Reconciliation** | Daily client-ledger against bank against depository reconciliation; **monthly client-level fund and securities balance upload within seven calendar days of month end**; holding statements; early-warning inputs | The monthly per-client reporting obligation and the early-warning mechanism |
| **Compliance** | The regulatory calendar; periodic submissions; policy maintenance; the certification register; internal-audit and inspection liaison; client-code modification control; advertisement and news approval | Client code modification; advertisement and terminal grants; unauthenticated news requiring compliance-officer approval; outsourcing; conflicts of interest |
| **Surveillance** | Client-behaviour alerts; mule-account detection; order-spoofing exposure; escalation to the exchange; the surveillance return | The institutional mechanism for prevention and detection of fraud or market abuse, and the designated-large-broker client-behaviour surveillance obligation |
| **Client service and grievance** | The exclusive complaints inbox; the grievance and dispute-resolution platforms; redressal within the prescribed period; **monthly complaint disclosure**; the investor charter | The grievance redressal obligations |
| **Technology and operations centre** | Capacity planning; logging and monitoring; the recovery site and **live recovery drills**; software testing and change management; technical-glitch detection and reporting; order-rate controls for programmatic access | Testing of software used in trading and risk management; safeguards against software-vendor failure; the technical-glitch framework; and the enhanced system-audit obligation to verify glitch reporting, capacity planning proportional to growth, change and patch management **including vendor-provided order and risk systems**, log preservation, that order-placing and routing servers are at the broker's premises, and recovery-site and live-drill compliance |
| **Internal audit liaison** | Managing the external half-yearly audit; the annual asset-inventory certification; tracking and closing action-taken reports; following up on regulator and exchange inspection findings | The internal audit obligations, the asset inventory submitted with the first-half report, and the duty on internal auditors to monitor corrective steps on inspection deficiencies |

<Aside type="caution" title="On headcount and shift patterns: the honest position">
**No sourced headcount bands or shift rosters were established**, and this page declines to invent them. What the regulation actually says is that resources must be *adequate*: a designated large broker must maintain adequate human resources, systems, processes and procedures, give staff the resources and support to carry out their duties, and train employees at regular intervals; members must ensure adequate human and technological resources to monitor authorised persons; and depository-participant surveillance requires approved procedures, maker-and-checker separation and resource adequacy.

What *can* be stated factually is the set of hard clocks that drive staffing: contract notes within **twenty-four hours** of execution; technical-glitch intimation to the exchange **immediately, and not later than a short fixed period** from occurrence, with a preliminary incident report the next day and a root-cause analysis within a defined number of working days; **cyber incident reporting within six hours** of detection; daily client-level collateral reporting; margin-trading gross-exposure disclosure by a fixed hour on the following trading day; monthly client-balance upload within **seven calendar days** of month end; and authorised-person incident escalation within **one working day**. A staffing model derived from those clocks is defensible. One derived from a number in a document is not.
</Aside>

## 7. Where the regulation prescribes a function and leaves the structure open

Stating this honestly is more useful than filling the gap with invention.

| Area | What is actually required | What is **not** mandated |
|---|---|---|
| Organisation structure | Identifiable accountability for each obligation | Desk names, reporting lines, or a chart |
| Headcount | Adequacy, with training at regular intervals | Any number, ratio or band |
| Shift pattern | Meeting the clocks | Any roster |
| **Dealing separated from risk management** | Required **in substance** through the internal-control, surveillance and fraud-prevention obligations, and tested by the internal audit under the effectiveness of internal control | **Not** located as a named clause. Do not cite it as one |
| **Payout maker separated from payout approver** | The same: a four-eyes control the rulebook reaches through internal control and the institutional mechanism | **Not** located as a named clause |
| Chief technology officer | Senior technology capability, and the technology committee's functions | The role itself |
| Annual compliance certificate | The specific periodic artefacts listed in the submissions calendar | A single annual compliance certificate |

<Aside type="caution" title="Why the distinction in the last two rows matters">
A compliance manual that asserts "the exchange requires dealing to be segregated from risk management" is making a claim an inspector can test and find unsupported, which damages the credibility of everything around it. The better formulation is that the firm maintains the separation as an internal control, that the control exists because the internal-control and fraud-prevention obligations require effective controls in substance, and that the internal auditor tests it. That formulation is accurate, is defensible, and leads to exactly the same control being implemented. The institutional mechanism introduced by the 2024 amendment and carried into the 2026 regulations is the closest thing to a codified four-eyes regime: it requires documented policies defining roles, responsibilities and alert thresholds, processes to detect mule accounts and suspicious activity, employees to inform senior management without delay, prompt notification to the exchange on detection, a documented whistle-blower policy with a confidential channel and protection against retaliation, quarterly control reviews, half-yearly summary analysis to the exchange, and at least annual system review.
</Aside>

## Practical notes

- **Keep the appointments register and the certification register separate and both live.** The first changes rarely and matters enormously; the second changes constantly and gates daily operations.
- **Track the revalidation window opening, not the expiry date.** It is the single change that prevents most certification lapses.
- **Give the compliance officer a reporting channel that management cannot gate.** The obligation assumes one; an org chart that routes everything through the chief executive defeats it.
- **Fill the cyber designated officer and technology committee roles on day one.** They apply to every broker, they are frequently missed in small firms, and the half-yearly review leaves minutes that an inspector looks for.
- **Derive staffing from the clocks.** The twenty-four-hour contract note, the six-hour cyber report and the seven-day monthly upload do more to determine a roster than any benchmark.
- **Do not let one person hold two appointments that are meant to check each other.** The regulation does not always forbid it; the purpose of the appointments usually does.
