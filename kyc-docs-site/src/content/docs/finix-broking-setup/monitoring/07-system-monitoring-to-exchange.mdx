> **Why this page is structured this way:** There is one question a firm needs answered before it designs anything here — **does the exchange watch my systems continuously, or does it only hear from me when something breaks?** The answer is not the same for every broker, and getting it wrong in either direction is expensive: a firm that assumes continuous monitoring builds a telemetry pipeline it does not owe, and a firm that assumes incident-only reporting discovers at designation that it must serve a named parameter set in real time. So section 2 answers it by category, before anything else. Sections 5 and 6 then record the genuine conflicts in the published material rather than resolving them silently.

## TL;DR

- **A broker outside the exchange-designated list pushes no continuous telemetry.** Its obligations are **incident reporting plus internal self-monitoring**. That is the answer for a greenfield firm.
- **Designated brokers do stream.** They stand up an interface gateway and make a named parameter set available, and the exchange independently monitors those parameters **in real time**.
- **Whether that is a pull or a push is genuinely unresolved in the primary material** — one annexure says the values are *served by* the broker, an audit term of reference says data *is published in* a monitoring server. Both are primary. This page flags it rather than choosing.
- **No exchange-installed agent appears in any primary source.** If a vendor proposes one, that is a vendor design, not a regulatory requirement.
- **The parameter count itself is inconsistent** — the instrument lists fourteen, the auditor's terms of reference carry two extra. Build to the superset.
- **No threshold values are published anywhere**, and no specific financial disincentive line exists for the parameters. The firm sets its own thresholds and must justify them.
- **The one continuous link a small broker has from day one is a security one** — the cyber framework routes smaller regulated entities onto an exchange-operated security operations centre. That is security telemetry, not performance.

## 1. Framework

- **Technical glitch framework of 25 November 2022** — the original, with intimation within **one hour**, a preliminary report the next day, and a root-cause analysis within **fourteen days**. `[not yet in index]`
- **Revised framework effective 9 January 2026** — intimation within **two hours**, a preliminary report the next day **with trading-holiday relief added**, and a root-cause analysis within **fourteen working days**. `[not yet in index]`
- **Exchange inspection master of April 2026**, whose submissions exhibit carries an item for **parameters for monitoring technical glitches** with a periodicity of **real time**. `[not yet in index]`
- **Exchange system-audit terms of reference of April 2026** — the auditor's scope over the monitoring mechanism, including the parameter list and the publication requirement. `[not yet in index]`
- **The cyber security and resilience framework** — categorisation of regulated entities, and the routing of smaller entities onto an exchange-operated security operations centre. `[not yet in index]`
- **Regulator press release of 2025** describing a **common reporting mechanism** that allows a common report to be filed at one exchange rather than at several, live from **1 August 2025**. `[not yet in index]`
- **Regulator circular of 24 August 2026** — cyber incident reporting aligned to a standard report format. `[not yet in index]`

## 2. The question, answered by category

| Category | Continuous telemetry to the exchange? | What is actually owed |
|---|---|---|
| **A broker outside the designated list** — including every greenfield firm | **No** | **Incident reporting** on the glitch and cyber clocks, **internal self-monitoring** of the parameter set, capacity planning, and the audit evidence that both exist |
| **A designated broker** | **Yes** | An **interface gateway** through which a **named parameter set is made available**, which the exchange **independently monitors in real time**, in addition to everything above |
| Every broker, for security | **Yes, in substance** | The cyber framework routes smaller regulated entities onto an **exchange-operated security operations centre** — a continuous link, but **security telemetry rather than system performance** |

<Aside type="danger" title="Designation is the switch, and it is not in the firm's control">
The difference between these two postures is a list the exchange publishes, not a size the firm chooses. A firm below the line owes self-monitoring and incident reporting; a firm on the list owes a real-time interface. The engineering implications are entirely different, and the transition is not gradual — you are off the list, then you are on it.

Two consequences for planning. **Build the parameter instrumentation from day one even while off the list**, because you owe the self-monitoring anyway and because the only thing designation then changes is exposure rather than measurement. And **treat the designated-broker interface as a known future project with a known trigger**, rather than something to discover when the list is published. The equivalent planning point for the enhanced-obligation regime generally is in [the 2026 regulatory change register](/broking-kyc/finix-broking-setup/compliance/06-regulatory-change-2026/).
</Aside>

## 3. The incident clocks

| Obligation | 2022 framework | **Revised, effective 9 January 2026** |
|---|---|---|
| Intimation to the exchange | **Within one hour** of occurrence | **Within two hours** |
| Preliminary incident report | Next day | Next day, **with trading-holiday relief added** |
| Root-cause analysis | Within **fourteen days** | Within **fourteen working days** |
| Cyber incident reporting | Within hours of detection, to the exchanges, the regulator and the national response team | Unchanged in timing; **aligned to a standard initial, intermediate and final report sequence** from August 2026 |

**The clock runs from occurrence or detection**, not from escalation — which is why the compliance officer must be reachable at any hour, since they file. The internal path is in [escalation and on-call](/broking-kyc/finix-broking-setup/roles/05-escalation-oncall/) and the full incident treatment in [incident and breach reporting](/broking-kyc/finix-broking-setup/monitoring/05-incident-breach-reporting/).

## 4. The reporting mechanism

| Element | Position |
|---|---|
| The common reporting mechanism | Described at regulator level as a **technology-based common reporting mechanism enabling a common report to be filed at one exchange instead of at several**, live from **1 August 2025** |
| Its name | **Verified at regulator level.** **The exchange's own circulars do not use that name** — they describe "technology-based sharing mechanisms of common submissions among exchanges" |
| **A portal address** | **Does not appear in any source examined.** Do not configure one from a secondary reference |
| What this means practically | Use the exchange's own described route and name, and treat the regulator-level name as the policy label rather than a system identifier |

## 5. The parameter set, and its conflicts

The monitoring mechanism has a named parameter set. Two things about it are inconsistent in the published material and both matter for implementation.

| Conflict | Detail | How to handle it |
|---|---|---|
| **Count** | The instrument lists **fourteen** parameters. The **auditor's terms of reference carry two additional ones** — a network latency measure and a name-resolution failure measure | **Build to the superset.** The auditor examines against their own list, so instrumenting only the shorter one produces a finding |
| **Pull or push** | One annexure describes the values as **served by** the broker, implying the exchange retrieves them. An audit term of reference asks whether data **is being published in** a monitoring server, implying the broker sends them | **Genuinely unresolved in primary material.** Design the gateway so it can do both — expose a retrievable endpoint and retain the ability to publish — and **obtain written clarification from the exchange** before committing to one |
| **Thresholds** | **No threshold values are published anywhere** | **The firm sets its own, documents the basis, and has it reviewed.** An unjustified threshold is the finding, not a wrong number |
| **A specific disincentive** | **No financial-disincentive line item exists for the parameters themselves** | The exposure is through the glitch framework and the inspection process, not a published per-parameter penalty |
| **An exchange-installed agent** | **Appears in no primary source** | If a vendor proposes one, it is a vendor design choice. Do not describe it as mandated |

<Aside type="caution" title="Why the pull-versus-push ambiguity is worth resolving in writing">
This is not an academic distinction. A **pull** model means the firm exposes an authenticated endpoint, the exchange calls it on its own schedule, and availability of that endpoint becomes a monitored dependency — a firm whose gateway is down has stopped serving even if its trading systems are healthy. A **push** model means the firm is responsible for delivery, retry, backfill after an outage, and the question of what happens to data generated while the link was down.

The security posture, the failure modes and the operational ownership all differ. Because both readings sit in current primary documents, the right action is a written question to the exchange rather than an inference, and the answer should be retained with the design. Building a gateway that can satisfy either is the cheap hedge in the meantime.
</Aside>

## 6. Two further discrepancies worth recording

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **"Top twenty" against the printed list** | A provision describing the designated population as the top twenty by client base **prints a list of forty-two names**. Do not compute your own designation from the stated rule — **use the published list** |
| Dead text in a current master | The exchange's current master still describes the **investor risk reduction platform**, which was **discontinued on 7 May 2026**. Keep it out of runbooks and out of any outage promise to clients |

The general lesson, which recurs across this manual: **a consolidated circular is not authority for the currency of everything in it.** See the change-management discipline in [the 2026 regulatory change register](/broking-kyc/finix-broking-setup/compliance/06-regulatory-change-2026/).

## 7. The real engineering burden

Not the telemetry. The hardest obligation in this area is **attribution of impact to clients**, for arbitrary windows, certified by an auditor and held immutably.

| Requirement | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Per-client impact attribution** | For an incident, the firm must be able to say **which clients were affected** |
| **For arbitrary short windows** | Impact attributable over a small window rather than a whole session |
| **Auditor-certified** | The method and the output are examined, not merely asserted |
| **Immutable retention over years** | The underlying data cannot be summarised away afterwards |
| What this means in design | Per-client, per-request outcome data at a granularity fine enough to window, with durable immutable storage sized for years — **a data-retention and lineage problem, not a monitoring one** |
| Where it lands architecturally | The event log, which is already a regulatory record for order and trade purposes — see [the front-end gateway and the event architecture](/broking-kyc/finix-broking-setup/stack/bff-and-event-architecture/) |

**This is the item most likely to be under-scoped.** Dashboards and alerts are straightforward; reconstructing, two years later, exactly which clients could not place an order during a particular few minutes is not, unless the data was captured at that granularity at the time.

## 8. Capacity planning

| Element | Position |
|---|---|
| The obligation | Capacity planned **proportionally to client and turnover growth** |
| Who checks | The **system auditor**, as an explicit scope item |
| What must exist | A documented plan, the basis of the projection, headroom measurement, and evidence that it is revisited as the business grows |
| What is examined alongside | Software testing and change management **including vendor-provided order and risk systems**, log preservation, that order-placing and routing servers are at the broker's premises, and recovery-site and live-drill compliance |
| The practical metric | **Headroom at peak**, measured rather than assumed, with the peak taken from observed load rather than a nominal figure |

## 12. Practical notes

- **Settle the designation question first.** Everything else here is conditional on it, and it comes from a published list rather than a calculation.
- **Instrument the superset of parameters from day one**, even off the list. You owe the self-monitoring, and designation then changes exposure rather than measurement.
- **Get the pull-versus-push answer in writing** and keep it with the design. Both readings are currently supportable.
- **Set thresholds and document why.** The absence of published values makes the justification the deliverable.
- **Scope the per-client impact attribution early.** It is a retention and lineage problem and it is the item most often under-estimated.
- **Do not describe an exchange-installed agent as required.** It appears nowhere in primary material.
- **Measure headroom against observed peak, not a nominal figure**, because that is what the auditor will ask about.
